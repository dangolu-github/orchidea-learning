Reading Task 1 · The Repair Café

Read the article and choose A, B or C for questions 1–10.

Exam move: Find the sentence that proves your answer. Reject an option if it is mentioned but changes the reason, number, time or result.

When the small town of Elmsford announced a monthly Repair Café, organiser Marta Varga expected people to bring lamps and torn shirts. She did not expect thirty-seven visitors on the first morning, or a queue outside the community hall before the doors opened. Some people wanted to save money, but many said they were simply tired of throwing useful things away.

The café opens on the first Saturday of each month from 9:30 a.m. to 1 p.m. Visitors book a thirty-minute slot online, although five places are kept for people who arrive without a booking. There is no fixed repair charge. Instead, visitors may make a small donation to cover materials such as thread, glue and electrical wire. Replacement parts that cost more than five euros must be paid for separately.

Twelve volunteers work at four tables: clothing, bicycles, small electrical items and wooden furniture. They do not simply take an object away and return it later. The owner stays beside the volunteer, learns what went wrong and helps with at least one step of the repair. This rule means fewer items can be handled, but Marta believes the lesson is more valuable than a quick service.

Not everything can be accepted. Volunteers will not open microwave ovens because some parts can remain dangerous even after the machine is unplugged. They also refuse items that are still covered by a shop guarantee, since an unofficial repair might cancel the owner’s right to a free replacement.

During the first six months, 214 objects were brought in and 153 were repaired. Another twenty-eight could not be fixed that day, but their owners received advice about the correct part or specialist. The team now plans short evening classes on basic sewing and bicycle care. Marta hopes that, eventually, visitors will arrive with fewer broken things because they know how to prevent simple problems.

Reading Task 2 · A Market’s Quiet Hour

Read the article and answer questions 1–10. Use no more than five words for each answer.

Exam move: Answer only the detail requested. Check whether the question asks for a time, person, reason, number or result.

Saturday morning used to be the busiest and noisiest period at North Street Market. Music played near the entrance, sellers called out special offers, and metal baskets knocked against the floor. For most customers, the lively atmosphere was part of the experience. For some autistic shoppers and people with anxiety, however, the lights and noise made an ordinary food shop exhausting.

After receiving a letter from a local parent group, manager Daniel Lee introduced a quiet hour from 8:30 to 9:30 every Saturday. During that time, the market turns off background music and reduces the brightness of half the ceiling lights. Staff do not use the public announcement system unless there is an emergency. Sellers may speak to customers, but they are asked not to shout across the hall.

The changes began as an eight-week trial. Before it started, every trader attended a forty-minute session led by an autism-support worker. They learned not to touch a customer unexpectedly and to allow extra time for a reply. The market also produced a simple map showing the toilets, exits and quieter corners. Customers can download it before their visit or collect a paper copy at the entrance.

At first, several traders worried that a quieter market would feel unfriendly and reduce sales. The opposite happened. Visitor numbers during the hour rose by nearly a third, while total Saturday sales stayed at their usual level. Many older shoppers also began arriving early because they found it easier to hear conversations.

Daniel says the project is not perfect. Deliveries sometimes arrive late and create sudden noise, and the fish seller cannot make the refrigeration equipment completely silent. The market has therefore added a notice near the entrance when unusual noise is expected. After six months, the quiet hour has become permanent. Daniel is now discussing a second weekly session on Wednesday afternoons.

Reading Task 3 · Saturday Volunteer Roles

Read roles A–E. For questions 1–10, choose the role that matches each person. You may use a role more than once.

Exam move: Match every condition, not just one keyword. Check age, time, language, training and physical demands before choosing.

ARiverside Trail Crew

Help clear leaves, paint signs and report damaged paths beside the river. The group works from 8 to 11 a.m. on the second Saturday of each month. Tools, gloves and a hot drink are provided. The work is outdoors and includes lifting light branches. Volunteers aged 14–17 are welcome with an adult. The session is cancelled in heavy rain.

BLibrary Digital Help Desk

Support adults who want to use email, video calls or online forms. You do not need to be a computer expert, but you must be patient and able to explain one step at a time. Volunteers work from 10 a.m. to 1 p.m. for six Saturdays. A short online training session takes place on the Thursday before the programme begins.

CCommunity Kitchen

Join a team preparing 120 takeaway meals for local families. Tasks include washing vegetables, measuring ingredients, packing food and cleaning work areas. The shift runs from 9:30 a.m. to 2:30 p.m. New helpers receive a food-safety briefing on arrival and must wear closed shoes. Volunteers must be at least 18. No driving is required.

DMuseum Story Team

Turn old letters and photographs into short, clear labels for a new town-history display. Volunteers meet at the museum from 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. once a month and complete about one hour of reading at home before each meeting. This quiet role suits people with strong written English who enjoy checking facts. Previous museum experience is not necessary.

EFamily Sports Welcome Crew

Greet families, show them where activities take place and answer simple questions at the Saturday sports programme. The shift is from 1 to 5 p.m. every two weeks. You will walk or stand for most of the afternoon. Basic Hungarian and English are needed. The role is unpaid, but local bus or tram costs are refunded.
