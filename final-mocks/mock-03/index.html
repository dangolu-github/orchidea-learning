A Choir for People Who Think They Cannot Sing

When music teacher Hannah Price announced a new community choir, her poster carried an unusual promise: no auditions and no need to read music. [R01] She had met many adults who loved music but had stopped singing after one unkind comment at school.

The first rehearsal attracted twenty-seven people. Hannah began with breathing games instead of a song. [R02] The room soon filled with laughter, which helped everyone relax. Only then did she teach a simple two-line melody.

During the first month, members always stood in a circle. [R03] It also meant that stronger singers could support less confident neighbours without anyone feeling watched. Hannah recorded each part and shared it privately, so members could practise at home. [R04]

The group faced its first real test when a care home invited them to perform. Several members wanted to refuse. [R05] Hannah suggested a shorter programme and reminded them that the audience had asked for enjoyment, not perfection. Eventually, everyone agreed.

On the day, the opening song started badly because the pianist missed a page. [R06] A choir member gave the note, and the group began again. The audience clapped to encourage them. After that, the singers forgot to be nervous.

The performance changed the choir. [R07] Membership grew to forty, and the group began collecting donations for local causes. They still do not hold auditions. [R08]

Hannah believes progress comes from regular practice and sensible choices. [R09] She also chooses songs that can be divided into easier and harder parts. This lets beginners take part while more experienced singers remain challenged.

The choir's next goal is an outdoor summer concert. Bad weather is possible, of course. [R10] For members who once refused to sing even in front of friends, making a back-up plan for a public concert feels like a remarkable success.

Reading Task 2 — True, false or not given

Read the article. Choose True, False or Not given for R11–R20.

The Two-Minute Conversation Project

A university survey found that first-year students often recognised many faces on campus but knew very few names. In response, the student union created the Two-Minute Conversation Project. Every Wednesday lunchtime, volunteers place bright green cards on selected café tables. Sitting at one of these tables means you are willing to have a brief conversation with someone new.

The idea came from nursing student Imani Bell, who had noticed that long social events could feel difficult for shy students. Two minutes, she thought, was short enough to feel safe but long enough to discover a shared interest. Participants use a small question card if they cannot think of a topic. Questions include “What useful thing did you learn this week?” and “Which place on campus helps you relax?” Politics and personal finances are deliberately avoided.

No one measures conversations with a timer. The name simply reminds people that they may leave politely after a short exchange. Some conversations end quickly, while others continue through lunch. Volunteers stay nearby to welcome new arrivals and explain the rule that a person can always choose not to answer a question.

During the first term, 312 students tried the tables. An anonymous follow-up form received 96 responses. Most respondents said the project made it easier to greet people later in class. Twelve reported meeting someone they now considered a close friend. The organisers were pleased but careful: because only some participants completed the form, they cannot claim that the results represent everyone.

Next term, the union plans to add one evening session for students who work during the day. It will keep the Wednesday tables as well. Several lecturers have asked for cards to use in lessons, but Imani wants the project to remain voluntary and student-led.
