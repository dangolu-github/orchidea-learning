The Saturday Repair Table

Two years ago, residents of Millbrook noticed a worrying change. Small electrical items, toys and clothes were being thrown away even when the damage was minor. The nearest repair shop had closed, and many people did not know how to fix things themselves. In response, retired engineer Daniel Cho and tailor Mina Patel placed two tables in the public library and invited neighbours to bring one broken object each.

At first, the organisers expected perhaps ten visitors. More than forty people arrived, carrying lamps, radios, coats and even a wooden train. The volunteers could not repair everything, but they showed owners how to investigate the problem safely. A teenager called Freya discovered that her desk lamp only needed a new plug. She later joined the team and now manages the booking list.

The event, now called the Saturday Repair Table, takes place once a month. Visitors book a twenty-minute slot online or ask the library staff to help them. The service is free, although people may need to buy replacement parts. Volunteers refuse jobs involving gas equipment or badly damaged batteries because specialist training is required. They also explain that there is no guarantee an item can be saved.

The project has produced benefits that nobody predicted. Older volunteers enjoy passing on practical skills, while younger residents help them use online manuals. People often stay after their appointment to watch another repair or share advice. According to the library, Saturday attendance has risen by a third. The council is now considering a second table in the north of the town.

Daniel insists that the project is not mainly about saving money. “A successful repair changes the way people see an object,” he says. “It becomes something with a history, not just something replaceable.” Still, he warns other towns not to copy the project too quickly. A safe workspace, clear rules and patient volunteers are more important than a large collection of tools.

Reading Task 2 — Short answers

Read the report. Answer R11–R20 in no more than four words and/or a number.

A Station Garden After Dark

Most commuters hurry through Westford Station without noticing the narrow strip of land behind platform three. Until last spring, it was covered with weeds and old packaging. Then station employee Rosa Mendes suggested turning it into a small wildlife garden. Her manager agreed on one condition: the work could not interrupt train services.

For this reason, the six-person volunteer team met after the final evening train every Tuesday. They wore bright safety jackets and stayed behind a marked fence. Their first task was not planting. They spent three weeks removing rubbish and testing the soil. When the tests showed that vegetables would not be suitable, they chose flowers that could support bees and butterflies instead.

A local primary school built four wooden insect hotels, while a garden centre donated tools. The volunteers bought the plants with money collected from a quiz night. They selected species that flower at different times, so insects can find food from March until October. Rainwater from the station roof is stored in two large containers and used during dry weather.

The garden officially opened in June. It has no public path because passengers are not allowed behind the platform fence, but they can see it clearly from trains waiting at the station. Rosa posts a monthly photo update on the station website. In the first three months, volunteers recorded seven kinds of butterfly, compared with only two before the work began.

The team hopes to add nesting boxes this winter. However, Rosa says the most important next step is finding two new volunteers. Night work is tiring, and one original member has moved away. Anyone interested must attend the station’s safety course before joining.
