Reading Task 1 · The Last Bus Home

Read the article and choose A, B or C for questions 1–10.

Exam move: Find the sentence that proves your answer. Reject an option if it is merely mentioned but changes the reason, time, number or condition.

For years, people who finished work late in Bellford had only two choices: pay for a taxi or walk home. The final public bus left the town centre at 10:15 p.m., forty-five minutes before the hospital’s evening shift ended. After a staff survey found that 62 per cent of late workers spent more than an hour travelling home, the council agreed to test a new service called NightLink.

NightLink uses two fourteen-seat electric minibuses. It operates from Monday to Saturday between 10:30 p.m. and 1:30 a.m. Passengers book a journey by 10 p.m., either through a simple app or by telephone. The buses use twelve fixed stops, but the order changes according to that evening’s bookings. This makes the journey more direct than an ordinary bus route. A ticket costs the same as a daytime bus ticket, and monthly passes are accepted. Drivers do not take cash, which the council says saves time and improves safety.

Anyone may use the service, although bookings from the hospital, railway depot and food factory are placed first when both buses are full. Some residents have criticised this rule. They argue that young people returning from evening courses also need reliable transport. Transport manager Elena Marsh says the priority rule is temporary and will be reviewed after the twelve-week trial.

The first month produced 420 passenger journeys. Most riders used NightLink at least twice a week, and the average wait after work fell to eighteen minutes. However, requests from villages outside Bellford sometimes made the route late. The council therefore limited bookings to places within six kilometres of the town centre.

Hospital assistant Kareem Ali says the change has made his week less stressful. “I used to spend nearly a quarter of my evening pay on taxis,” he explains. “Now I know what time I will get home.” The council will continue NightLink if it carries an average of eight passengers each night and receives positive feedback from at least three quarters of users.

Reading Task 2 · A Town Counts Its Swifts

Read the article and answer questions 1–10. Use no more than five words for each answer.

Exam move: Give only the information requested. A short correct answer is safer than copying a whole sentence.

Every spring, swifts return to the town of Marston after spending the winter in Africa. These small dark birds rarely land except when they are nesting. For many years, they made their homes in narrow spaces under the roofs of old buildings. As damaged roofs were repaired, however, some of these entrances were closed. Local birdwatcher Nina Cole worried that the town was losing nesting places without realising it.

Nina formed the Marston Swift Team with twelve volunteers. From May to July, pairs of volunteers visit twelve locations at sunset twice a week. They do not try to count every bird in the sky. Instead, they watch for swifts entering a particular hole, because this is stronger evidence of an active nest. One person observes while the other records the exact time and address. They enter the information into an online map after returning home.

The survey brought surprises. Volunteers had expected the church tower to be the most important site, but no birds entered it. The roof above Patel’s Bakery, in contrast, contained six active nests. The team worked with the bakery owner to protect the openings during repairs. It also installed thirty wooden nest boxes on the library and sports hall. Small speakers played recorded swift calls for one hour after sunrise in May, helping returning birds notice the unfamiliar boxes.

The project also changed local building advice. Before roof work begins, owners are now asked to arrange a short nest survey. If swifts are present, builders can keep the original entrance or place a nest box nearby. Work close to an active nest must wait until the young birds have left.

At the end of the first summer, the team recorded eighteen occupied sites, compared with fourteen at the start. Nina is pleased but careful. A cold, wet June may have changed the birds’ behaviour, so one season cannot prove that the population has grown. The volunteers plan to repeat the same counts for five years. Their long-term goal is not simply to produce a bigger number, but to learn which actions genuinely help the birds return.
